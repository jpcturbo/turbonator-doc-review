T-LM Engine Modifications Tuning Guide

A practical, real-world calibration guide for Chrysler Turbo Logic Module (T-LM / Turbonator) applications. While MPTune automatically handles the basic math for injector scaling and MAP sensor conversion slopes, it does not perform the hands-on fine tuning, nor does it have built-in features for exhaust, throttle body, or camshaft upgrades. This guide breaks down each modification individually: which tables and constants to adjust in MPTune, why they need to change, and what values work best.

Hardware: Chrysler 2.2L & 2.5L Turbo (T1 / T2) ECU: Turbo Logic Module (T-LM) Tuning Tools: MPTune & MPScan Format: Practical Tuner Reference

Automated MPTune Setup vs. Hands-On Fine Tuning

When setting up a calibration for a modified car in MPTune:

What MPTune Does For You

  • Basic Injector Rescaling: Scales down the base fuel pulsewidth tables (WOTTBL, MAPTBL, and FuelBaselineFromMAP) proportionately based on old vs. new injector flow ratings.
  • Basic MAP Sensor Rescaling: Updates the voltage-to-pressure slope constants (MAPMULT and MAPADD) and stretches the pressure axes on your fuel, spark, and boost tables.

What You Need to Tune by Hand

  • Injector Latency / Dead Time (BATOFF): Injector opening delays do not scale with flow rate.
  • Adaptive Learning Floor (PWMIN): Keeping cruise pulse widths within the ECU's adaptive learning window.
  • Part-Throttle Boost Fueling (FPWUNK / PTPWOF): Rescaling the boost-onset fuel adder slope for 3-bar MAP conversions and larger injectors.
  • High-Boost Ignition Timing: MPTune leaves timing flat above 14.7 psi; higher boost requires pulling timing out to avoid knock.
  • Physical Airflow & Exhaust Changes: Fast spool-up, boost creep, throttle tip-in response, and camshaft idle vacuum.

1. Larger Exhaust & Catalytic Converter Removal

Physical modifications: 2.5" or 3" downpipe, high-flow cat or cat delete, free-flowing exhaust.
Exhaust & Boost Control

What Happens to the Engine

The factory exhaust on Chrysler turbo cars flows reasonably well, but opening it up to 2.5" or 3" and removing the catalytic converter reduces backpressure behind the turbo. This frees up extra horsepower and improves turbo spool-up response:

  • Slightly Quicker Spool: Boost builds 200 to 400 RPM sooner, and the turbo feels more eager to respond to throttle input.
  • Factory Boost Control vs. Manual Boost Controllers: The factory ECU boost control routine generally manages a free-flowing exhaust without drama. If you are using a manual boost controller (grainger valve / ball-and-spring), boost is regulated mechanically under the hood, and ECU duty cycle tables are not used. If using factory ECU solenoid control, you may just notice a slight 1–2 psi boost overshoot when the turbo first spools, which is easy to trim out.
  • Improved Top-End Breathing: Lower exhaust backpressure helps clear spent exhaust gases out of the cylinders during valve overlap, improving volumetric efficiency at higher RPM (4,500+ RPM) and slightly reducing combustion chamber heat.

✓ Check High-RPM AFR: Do a quick 3rd gear pull and watch your wideband. If AFR leans out slightly past 5,000 RPM due to the extra breathing, simply add 2% to 4% in PEFTBL at the top RPM points.

2. Bigger Throttle Body (52mm / 58mm vs. Stock 46mm)

Physical modifications: 52mm (Mopar / Super 60), 58mm (minivan swap), or custom aftermarket throttle body.
Transient Fuel & Airflow

What Happens to the Engine

A 52mm throttle body has 28% more flow area than stock, and a 58mm has 59% more area. Because the opening is so much larger, cracking the throttle just a few degrees dumps a massive surge of air into the intake manifold:

  • Severe Lean Tip-In Stumble (The Bog): When you quickly tap or press the throttle, manifold vacuum drops to atmospheric pressure almost instantly. The engine gets an immediate gulp of air before the fuel system can catch up, causing a momentary lean condition (16:1–18:1 AFR) and a noticeable stumble or bog.
  • Higher Idle Air Leakage: A larger throttle plate has a larger circumference. Small gaps around the butterfly allow more air into the engine when the throttle is closed, which can cause the idle to hang high unless base idle airflow is readjusted.
  • Full Airflow Reached Early: With a 52mm or 58mm throttle body, the intake plenum reaches zero vacuum (atmospheric pressure) at only 40% to 55% throttle pedal travel rather than 75% to 80%.
Understanding Pumpshot (Accelerator Pump Enrichment)
In Chrysler turbo ECUs, there are two separate pumpshot calculations:
1. TPS-Based Pumpshot (POSSLP & THRMAX): Reacts immediately to how fast the throttle pedal is moving.
2. MAP-Based Pumpshot (AESLOP & MAPMAX): Reacts to how fast manifold vacuum drops.
When you install a bigger throttle body, increasing TPS-based pumpshot (POSSLP) is the primary cure for off-idle hesitation.

Setting Mechanical Base Idle on a Bigger Throttle Body

  1. Get the engine fully warmed up to operating temp. Unplug the 4-wire AIS motor connector while running.
  2. Adjust the throttle stop screw until base idle sits around 650–700 RPM.
  3. Check your TPS voltage in MPScan with throttle closed. It should sit between 0.60V and 0.90V.
  4. Shut off the engine, plug the AIS back in, and disconnect the battery for 60 seconds to reset minimum throttle learned voltage.

3. Bigger Injectors (+20%, +40%, 72 pph) [Fine-Tuning Guidelines]

Injector sizes: +20% (~42 lb/hr), +40% (~52–55 lb/hr Bosch/Siemens), and 72 pph (~750–800 cc/min large format).
Fine-Tuning Only (MPTune Handles Base Scale)

What MPTune Automatically Scales vs. What Needs Fine Tuning

In MPTune, using Scaling → Injectors → Size scales down your fuel tables (WOTTBL, MAPTBL, and FuelBaselineFromMAP). Many cars run for years with just this basic scaling. However, for a truly clean, factory-smooth idle, steady closed-loop operation, and crisp off-idle manners, there are four key areas every tuner should keep in mind:

  • Injector Latency / Dead Time (BATOFF): Flow rate scales, but mechanical opening and closing dead time does not. At warm idle on big injectors, the total pulsewidth is very short (1.1–1.5 ms). If the dead time is off even slightly, idle will run noticeably richer or leaner than intended, especially when battery voltage changes (like when the radiator fan kicks on).
  • Adaptive Learning Pulsewidth Floor (PWMIN): The ECU has a safety floor setting designed to stop it from learning adaptive trims during decel when pulsewidths are near zero. With large injectors (+40% or 72#), your normal cruising pulsewidth drops down into the 2.2–3.2 ms range. Scaling PWMIN down ensures the ECU recognizes normal cruising as valid running time and allows adaptive fuel trims to update smoothly.
  • Closed-Loop O2 Ramp Slopes (SECRMP / PRIRMP): Because larger injectors deliver much more fuel per millisecond, the ECU's continuous O2 integration ramp (SECRMP) must be scaled down (from +3/-3 counts to +1/-1 counts) to prevent the wideband from aggressively hunting around 14.7:1 at idle and cruise.
  • Part-Throttle Boost Fuel Adder (FPWUNK / PTPWOF): The boost-onset fuel offset constant (FPWUNK in stock calibrations, PTPWOF in Turbonator Stage IV) adds supplemental pulsewidth when manifold pressure exceeds ~5 psi under partial throttle. Because larger injectors spray significantly more fuel per millisecond, this constant must be divided by your injector flow multiplier (e.g. divide by 1.20 for +20% or 1.40 for +40%) to prevent rich bogging and black smoke when rolling into boost on the highway.

Dialing in BATOFF by Watching Battery Voltage

To verify your BATOFF dead time setting: Warm the car to operating temp and let it idle. Watch your wideband AFR while turning on high electrical loads (headlights, blower motor on high, radiator fan). If the AFR goes lean when voltage drops, your latency value at 12–13V is too low (increase BATOFF slightly). If the AFR goes rich when voltage drops, your latency value is too high (decrease BATOFF).

4. 3-Bar and 4-Bar MAP Sensors [Fine-Tuning Guidelines]

Sensor upgrades: Mopar Super 60 / GM 3-Bar (~29 psi boost) and GM / Omnipower 4-Bar (~44 psi boost).
Fine-Tuning Only (MPTune Handles Scaling)

What Happens When You Go to a Larger MAP Sensor

The Chrysler computer uses an 8-bit sensor input with 256 discrete digital steps across the 0–5 Volt range. When you install a 3-bar or 4-bar sensor, you are stretching that same 0–5V range over twice as much pressure:

  • Coarser Vacuum Resolution: On a 2-bar sensor, each digital step is about 0.11 psi. On a 3-bar it is 0.17 psi, and on a 4-bar it is 0.23 psi. In deep idle vacuum (14 to 20 inHg), the engine's entire idle and light cruise range lives in a narrow band of only 25 to 35 steps.
  • Vacuum Step Jitter: If fuel or spark tables have steep ramps in the idle vacuum region, a 1-step sensor flicker can cause timing or fuel to jump, resulting in a hunting idle or surging cruise. Smoothing these areas cures it.
  • Part-Throttle Boost Fuel Rate Slope (FPWUNK / PTPWOF): Because a 3-bar sensor spreads 0–44.1 psia over the same 0–5V range as a 2-bar sensor (0–29.4 psia), each digital step represents 50% more boost, so the computer counts delta MAP 1.5 times slower. To deliver the proper fuel adder per pound of boost, FPWUNK / PTPWOF must be scaled up by 1.5 (+50%), and OBCONST must be reduced from 170 down to 113 to keep the boost trigger at ~5 psi.
Important Tuning Rule: Retard Ignition Timing in High Boost!
When you select 3-bar or 4-bar in MPTune, MPTune stretches the pressure axis of your spark table, but it keeps the timing flat above 14.7 psi.
That means whatever advance you had at 14 psi (typically 18° to 22°) will still be delivered at 25 or 30 psi! You must manually add points to your WOTMAP table to retard timing as boost climbs, keeping the engine safe from detonation.

5. Generic Larger Camshaft (Higher Duration & Lift)

Camshaft upgrades: Mopar Super 60, FWD-P Enforcer / F-Series, Taft, or custom turbo grinds.
VE Rotation & Idle Vacuum

What Happens to the Engine

Installing a larger camshaft with more duration and lift increases valve overlap. This causes noticeable physical changes:

  • Lower Idle Vacuum: Increased overlap causes exhaust reversion at low engine speeds. Idle vacuum drops from stock 18–20 inHg down to 11–14 inHg (or even 10 inHg on radical grinds).
  • The "Cruise Cell at Idle" Issue: Because the ECU reads 16-14 inHg at idle, it thinks the car is cruising under light load. It looks up cruise fuel and spark tables, dumping too much fuel and delivering sluggish timing. This causes a hunting, rolling idle and can foul plugs.
  • Volumetric Efficiency Shifts Higher: The cam sacrifices low-end efficiency (below 2,800 RPM) to make huge breathing gains at high RPM (above 4,500 RPM).
  • Valvetrain Noise & False Knock: Stiff valve springs, aggressive lobes, and roller followers create mechanical valvetrain noise that the factory knock sensor can mistake for real detonation.
The Classic "PEFTBL Rotation" Rule
Tuning for a performance cam in Chrysler turbo engines is universally described as rotating the Pumping Efficiency table (PEFTBL) counter-clockwise:
• Low RPM (1,200–2,800 RPM): Drop values by 4% to 8% (cures soggy throttle response and rich loading up).
• Mid RPM (3,500–4,000 RPM): Leave near stock.
• High RPM (4,800–6,500 RPM): Raise values by 6% to 12%+ (provides the extra fuel the cam breathes in at high RPM).

Listening for Real Knock vs. Valvetrain Noise

Before raising DLTKNK, build a simple mechanical det-can using a length of copper tube bolted to the cylinder head connected with fuel hose to sound earmuffs. Mechanical valvetrain noise sounds like a fast sewing machine clatter. Real detonation sounds like sharp pebbles rattling in a tin can. Raise DLTKNK only enough to ignore the valvetrain clatter while preserving real knock detection.
